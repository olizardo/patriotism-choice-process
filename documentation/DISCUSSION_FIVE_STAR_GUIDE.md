### **Improvement #3: Enhance the Hochschild Move with Greater Sociological Depth**

**Current (Line 199-200):**
```
Given that the usual impression is that low-status groups are the most likely 
to engage in overt displays of nationalism, an important analytical distinction, 
drawing on Hochschild's classic work \citeyearpar{hochschild1979emotion-624}, 
must therefore be maintained between class-based \textit{expression rules} and 
internal \textit{feeling rules}. While lower-status individuals may have been 
more likely to \textit{express} national pride in highly visible ways (e.g., 
via bumper stickers or overt patriotic displays), they were paradoxically less 
likely as a group to experience a proportional \textit{increase} in affective 
attachment. High-status individuals embedded in nested groups were actually 
more likely to \textit{feel} increased national pride after 9/11, even if they 
were less likely to outwardly express it in stereotypical ways. By shifting 
the theoretical lens to the choice process, I arrive at a better understanding 
of the structural and affective foundations of modern patriotism.
```

**Issue:** The Hochschild distinction is explained, but:
- The sociological implication isn't fully developed
- Why does this matter theoretically? (beyond reconciling contradiction)
- What does this tell us about how status/education shapes emotional experience?

**Revised:**
```
This analytical distinction, drawing on Hochschild's \citeyearpar{hochschild1979emotion-624} 
classic work on the managed emotions of emotional labor, is crucial for 
understanding the findings. Hochschild distinguishes between \textit{expression 
rules}---the social norms governing how emotions should be displayed in public 
contexts---and \textit{feeling rules}---the internal, internalized standards 
for what one ought to feel. In the American context, there is a persistent 
class-inflected expression rule: lower-status groups, particularly working-class 
and rural Americans, face stronger normative pressure to display patriotic 
sentiment through visible symbols (flag displays, bumper stickers, patriotic 
rhetoric). Yet feeling rules operate differently.

My results suggest that post-9/11, it was precisely high-status, educated 
individuals embedded in voluntary associations who experienced proportionally 
the largest \textit{increases} in felt national pride---even as they remained 
less likely to adopt stereotypical patriotic performances. Meanwhile, lower-status 
individuals, constrained by more rigid class-based expression rules, may have 
intensified their patriotic performances without correspondingly increasing 
their internal affective attachment. This is not a failure of the needs-based 
perspective to explain feeling, but rather evidence that its mechanism operates 
primarily at the level of behavioral/cultural conformity rather than emotional 
experience. By contrast, the choice-process theory directly predicts affective 
transformation, which is exactly what the data show: differential feeling rules 
operate as a function of differential structural autonomy.
```

**Why This Works:**
- Defines Hochschild's distinction clearly (expression rules vs. feeling rules)
- Explains the CLASS component (why this varies by status)
- Reinterprets the findings sociologically (not just reconciling a contradiction)
- Shows how choice-process is superior: it predicts the right mechanism (affective vs. behavioral)
- Demonstrates sophisticated understanding of emotion sociology

**Length:** This is longer, but it's because it's doing more theoretical work.

### **Improvement #5: Strengthen the Limitations Section with More Philosophical Depth**

**Current (Line 201-209):**
The limitations section is honest and specific, which is good. But it reads more like a checklist than an integrated set of constraints on the interpretation.

**Revision Strategy:** Reorganize limitations into a more narrative structure that shows how they interact, rather than just listing them.

**Current Structure:**
- Limitation 1: Cross-sectional data (can't track individuals)
- Limitation 2: Can't include political ideology directly
- Limitation 3: Using proxies for autonomy (education, memberships)
- Limitation 4: 9/11 confounded with Iraq War

**Revised Structure (More Integrated):**

Replace the four separate limitation paragraphs with this revised version:

```
\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}

While this study leverages a unique historical juncture to evaluate macro-level 
shifts in attachment, the research design carries specific constraints that 
bound the conclusions. Three interconnected limitations warrant explicit discussion.

First, the analysis relies on pooled cross-sectional data from the GSS. Because 
I observe different, independent samples in 1996 and 2004, I cannot track true 
within-individual changes in national pride. This design constraint means I 
cannot definitively show that the same educated individual became more patriotic, 
only that aggregated patterns shifted. Future research with longitudinal panel 
data could observe intra-individual shifts in attribution rules and affective 
attachments following national crises, allowing researchers to make robust causal 
claims about how an individual's pre-crisis structural position conditions their 
post-crisis emotional response. More ambitiously, researchers could experimentally 
manipulate the salience of national symbols or induce experiences of autonomy in 
laboratory settings to directly test the causal mechanisms proposed here.

Second, I rely on educational attainment and voluntary association counts as 
proxy measures for structural autonomy and self-direction. While these proxies 
are theoretically justified and empirically robust, they are not identical to 
the underlying theoretical constructs. Future studies could directly measure 
occupational autonomy, subjective self-efficacy, and experienced agency via 
validated survey instruments. Similarly, data availability constraints prevented 
the inclusion of political ideology in the primary 2004 models, though 
supplementary analyses on the 1996 subsample and the robustness of 2004 results 
to nationalist sentiment suggest this omission does not drive the conclusions. 
Nonetheless, future investigations should leverage datasets where political 
conservatism is directly measured alongside structural measures, enabling 
researchers to examine the independent and interacting roles of political and 
structural predictors of national attachment across time.

Third, the temporal scope creates ambiguity about the mechanisms at work. The 
2004 wave was collected more than two years after September 11, encompassing 
the Afghanistan invasion, the buildup to Iraq, and the early years of the Iraq 
War. I do not view this as a threat to the theoretical argument, since the 
choice-process framework locates the mechanism in the cumulative salience of 
national rituals and symbols across the post-9/11 period rather than in 9/11 
as a punctual event. From this perspective, the wars supplied precisely this 
kind of salience-raising national symbolism and were integral to the mechanism, 
not confounds external to it. However, this reframing does create ambiguity: 
we cannot cleanly disentangle the effect of the attacks themselves from the 
effect of the subsequent period of wartime mobilization. Future work with more 
temporally granular data---for example, repeated cross-sections spanning 
2001--2004---could help parse these contributions and trace the trajectory of 
attribution rule shifts over time.

Together, these limitations suggest that while the findings provide strong 
support for the choice-process theory of national attachment, the specific 
mechanisms and boundary conditions require further investigation. The natural 
experiment provided by 9/11 is powerful but not perfect; future research must 
engage with more refined methods and data structures to fully specify how 
structural autonomy translates into affective attachment to national 
collectivities.
```

**Why This Works:**
- Integrates three limitations into one coherent argument rather than listing
- Shows how limitations interact (cross-sectional + proxies + temporal scope)
- Each limitation is paired with a specific future research direction
- Ends with a synthetic paragraph that acknowledges what we've learned and what remains
- Moves from "here's what's wrong" to "here's what's needed next"
